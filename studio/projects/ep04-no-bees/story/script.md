# Ink & Pip — Episode 04: "What If All the Bees Disappeared?"
Full episode · 19.11.2026 · EN dialogue · fast pace (Kokoro ×1.25/×1.28) · style: inkworld · rubric: animals & nature
Locations: ROOM (day, breakfast table) · MEADOW (flowers, bees, hive) · FLOWER (macro, UV view) · ORCHARD (blossoming trees) · MARKET (fruit stall) · FARM (cows, clover, cotton).

## 0:00 — HOOK
[ROOM day, breakfast table: apple, honey jar, blueberries, toast. The bees vanish; the apple, honey and berries pop away. The toast stays.]
PIP: If every bee on Earth vanished tonight, what would disappear from your breakfast first?
INK: Look at your plate.
PIP: My apple! My honey! My blueberries! Where did they go?
INK: That's the bees' work. They don't just make honey. They help make a huge part of what you eat.
PIP: But my toast is still here. Ha!
INK: For now. Let's find out how deep this goes.

## 0:20 — TITLE
[MEADOW: wide shot of flowers and buzzing bees. Title: WHAT IF ALL THE BEES DISAPPEARED?]
INK: Tonight we delete every bee on the planet. All of them, at once. Then we follow what happens: the first morning, the orchards, the supermarket, and the whole food chain.
PIP: And at the end?
INK: At the end, we find out who would have to do the bees' job. Spoiler: it's us.

## 0:40 — THE FIRST MORNING
[MEADOW: the bees vanish with a pop; the flowers are suddenly quiet.]
PIP: Okay, the bees are gone. What's the first thing I'd notice?
INK: Silence. A summer meadow without buzzing. And the flowers wouldn't know anything is wrong yet.
PIP: Wait, how many bees are we talking about? Like, a few hives?
INK: Way more. Scientists know more than twenty thousand species of bees. The honeybee is just one of them.
PIP: Twenty thousand kinds? I thought a bee was a bee!
INK: Most bees don't live in hives at all. They live alone, in tiny holes in the ground or in old wood. Most of them never make honey, and many of them can barely sting.
PIP: So bees are mostly quiet loners. Like you.
INK: Exactly like me. And we do important work.

## 1:20 — WHAT BEES ACTUALLY DO
[FLOWER macro: a giant flower, pollen grains, a fuzzy bee carrying them.]
PIP: Okay, but what is that important work? Bees just drink flower juice.
INK: You see, a flower has a problem. To make a fruit and seeds, it needs pollen from another flower. But flowers can't walk.
PIP: So they order delivery.
INK: Exactly. The flower pays the bee with sweet nectar, and while the bee is drinking, pollen sticks to its fuzzy body. Then the bee flies to the next flower and drops some of it off. That's pollination.
PIP: So every apple starts with a bee delivery.
INK: Lots of them. And bees are really good at it. Their hairy bodies even carry a little static electricity, so pollen jumps onto them like dust onto a balloon.
PIP: Bees are tiny fuzzy balloons. I love it.
INK: And flowers help them. Bees can see ultraviolet light. Many flowers have hidden ultraviolet patterns, like landing lights, pointing straight to the nectar.
[FLOWER: the view switches to ultraviolet; a glowing target appears in the middle.]
PIP: Flowers have secret runways? That's so cool.
INK: Runways nobody uses anymore. Without bees, many flowers wait, and wait, and never make seeds.

## 2:05 — THE HONEY AND THE DANCE
[MEADOW: close to the hive, bees walk on the comb; one does a waggle dance.]
PIP: Okay, but the honey. Tell me about the honey.
INK: Honey is the bees' food for winter. A honeybee makes about one twelfth of a teaspoon of honey in her whole life.
PIP: Her whole life? That's one tiny spoon!
INK: One tiny drop. A jar of honey is the life's work of hundreds of bees.
PIP: Now I feel bad for eating it.
INK: Then eat it slowly. And honey almost never goes bad. Archaeologists have found honey in ancient Egyptian tombs that was thousands of years old.
PIP: Ancient honey? Would you eat it?
INK: Scientists say it could still be safe. I'd still say no thanks.
PIP: And how do bees find the flowers? Do they have maps?
INK: Better. When a honeybee finds great flowers, she flies home and dances. A wiggly run, again and again. The direction of the run tells the others which way to fly, and how long she wiggles tells them how far.
PIP: Bees give directions by dancing? I'd get lost so fast.
INK: A scientist named Karl von Frisch figured that out, and he won a Nobel Prize for it in 1973.
PIP: A Nobel Prize for watching bees dance. That's my dream job.

## 2:30 — THE ORCHARD
[ORCHARD: rows of trees in blossom, a few lonely flies.]
PIP: Okay, so which foods are in trouble?
INK: Scientists looked at the world's most important crops. Out of a hundred and fifteen of them, eighty-seven get help from animals that carry pollen. And bees are the biggest helpers by far.
PIP: Eighty-seven! That's almost all of them!
INK: Apples, cherries, almonds, blueberries, pumpkins, melons, avocados. In the United States, more than half of all the bee colonies that farmers rent go to one crop: the almond orchards of California.
PIP: The bees go on a road trip for almonds?
INK: Millions of hives, on trucks, every spring. And some fruits need a very special trick. Bumblebees grab a tomato flower and shake it, really fast, until the pollen falls out.
PIP: The bee does a little dance on the flower?
INK: Buzz pollination. Honeybees can't do it. So greenhouses all over the world use bumblebees to grow tomatoes.

## 3:30 — mid-roll
[ORCHARD: the blossoms slowly fall.]

## 3:30 — THE SUPERMARKET
[MARKET: a fruit stall full of crates. One by one, crates empty.]
PIP: Okay, let's go shopping in a world without bees.
INK: Let's. Scientists calculated what happens if all the animals that pollinate crops disappeared. The world would lose about a fifth of its fruit.
PIP: A fifth? Like every fifth apple, gone?
INK: Roughly. About twenty-three percent of the fruit, sixteen percent of the vegetables, and twenty-two percent of the nuts and seeds.
PIP: My almonds! My cherries! My guacamole!
INK: The shelves wouldn't be empty. But the colorful part of the store would shrink, and what's left would cost a lot more.
PIP: What about coffee? My mom can't live without her coffee.
INK: Coffee flowers can pollinate themselves, but when bees visit, the plants grow more coffee beans. So coffee would get rarer and more expensive too.
PIP: Okay. Nobody tell Mom.
PIP: So who cares? I'll just eat bread and pasta.
INK: Funny you should say that, because there's a famous scary quote about this.

## 4:15 — MYTH: FOUR YEARS LEFT
[MARKET: a big poster with a fake quote; label MYTH.]
PIP: Oh, I know it! Einstein said that if the bees disappear, humans have only four years left!
INK: That's the myth. There's no evidence Einstein ever said it. The quote first shows up in a French magazine in 1965. Einstein died ten years earlier.
PIP: So someone just borrowed his hair.
INK: Borrowed his famous name. And the four years part is wrong too. The biggest foods on Earth, wheat, rice and corn, are pollinated by the wind. No bees needed.
PIP: So humans wouldn't die out.
INK: No. Myth busted. But wait, the story isn't over.

## 4:50 — THE HIDDEN CHAIN
[FARM: cows in a field of clover and alfalfa, a cotton plant, a T-shirt.]
PIP: There's more? I still have my bread!
INK: Look at the cows. They eat clover and alfalfa, and farmers need bees to grow the seeds for those plants. Fewer seeds, less cow food.
PIP: Less cow food means...
INK: Less milk, less cheese, less ice cream.
PIP: Not the ice cream!
INK: And cotton plants grow more cotton when bees visit them. So even your T-shirt has a little bee in it.
PIP: My shirt is part bee? I'm wearing a bee?
INK: In a way. But here's the serious part. Fruit, vegetables and nuts are where we get lots of our vitamins. One big study estimated that without pollinators, more than a million extra people could die every year from diseases linked to a worse diet.
PIP: Whoa. So the bees don't keep us alive. They keep us healthy.
INK: Exactly. Bread keeps you full. Fruit keeps you strong.

## 5:50 — THE CHOCOLATE QUESTION
[ORCHARD at the edge of a rainforest: a cacao tree with pods on the trunk; a tiny midge.]
PIP: Okay, I need to ask the most important question. What about chocolate?
INK: Good news. Chocolate doesn't need bees.
PIP: Yes! Wait, really?
INK: The cacao flower is tiny and complicated. Bees can't get into it. It's pollinated by flies called midges, about one millimeter long.
PIP: A fly the size of a dot saves chocolate?
INK: It does. And it's not very good at it. Only about three cacao flowers out of every thousand turn into a pod.
PIP: Three out of a thousand? Midges, you have one job!
INK: And they're the only ones who can do it. So chocolate survives. Your toast survives. But your fruit bowl is in trouble.

## 6:35 — TWIST: HUMAN BEES
[ORCHARD: people on ladders with brushes and little pots of pollen, touching every flower.]
PIP: Okay, what if we just do the bees' job ourselves?
INK: That's the twist. People already do. In some apple and pear orchards in Sichuan, in China, wild bees became so rare that farmers started pollinating by hand.
PIP: By hand? Every single flower?
INK: Every single flower. They climb ladders with a brush and a little pot of pollen and touch the flowers one by one. One person can only finish a handful of trees in a day.
PIP: And a bee hive?
INK: A healthy hive can visit more than a million flowers in a single day. For free.
PIP: Okay, the bees are way cheaper than us.
INK: And there's another hand-pollinated food you know. Vanilla. In 1841, on the island of Réunion, a twelve-year-old boy named Edmond Albius invented a quick way to pollinate vanilla flowers by hand. Vanilla farmers still use his trick today.
PIP: A twelve-year-old saved vanilla ice cream? Legend.
INK: Total legend. But there are not enough people on Earth to do every bee's job.
PIP: Then robots! Tiny robot bees!
INK: Engineers have built them. One robot insect from Harvard weighs less than a tenth of a gram, and it can flap its wings and fly.
PIP: Problem solved!
INK: Not even close. Most of them still need a wire for power, and we would need billions of them. Real bees build themselves, fix themselves, and run on flower juice.
PIP: Okay. Real bees win again.

## 7:25 — THE GOOD NEWS
[MEADOW: the bees pop back; flowers glow.]
PIP: Okay, I'm worried now. Are the bees actually disappearing?
INK: They're not all vanishing tonight. But many kinds of bees are in trouble, because they're losing flowers, homes, and clean places to live.
PIP: So what can we do? I can't build a hive.
INK: You don't have to. Plant some flowers. Leave a messy, wild corner in the garden. Let the dandelions grow. A lot of bees just need a place to live and something to eat.
PIP: What if I only have a balcony?
INK: Even one flower pot helps. Bees are always looking for a snack on the way.
PIP: Messy gardens save the world. My mom is going to love this.
INK: Probably not. But the bees will.

## 7:55 — OUTRO
[ROOM day: the apple, honey and blueberries pop back onto the table.]
PIP: So bees aren't just honey makers.
INK: Nope. They're the delivery service of the whole planet. Fruit, nuts, cow food, even your T-shirt.
PIP: Then let's give them a nice messy garden.

## Fact sheet
- >20,000 described bee species worldwide; most are solitary (no hive), many nest in the ground; most do not make honey (WWF "Five myths about bees"; USDA Forest Service "Bee pollination").
- Bees carry a positive electrostatic charge; flowers are negative → pollen is attracted (Clarke et al., Science 2013; Vaknin et al. 2000).
- Bees see ultraviolet (not red); many flowers have UV "nectar guides" (USDA Forest Service).
- Klein et al. 2007 (Proc. R. Soc. B): 87 of 115 leading global food crops benefit from animal pollination; ~35 % of global food production volume comes from such crops. Wheat, rice, maize are wind-pollinated.
- Almonds: 61 % of all US honeybee colony rentals (2016, USDA ERS Amber Waves 2018); 1.5–2+ million colonies trucked to California each Feb–Mar.
- Buzz pollination (sonication): bumblebees vibrate flowers of tomatoes, blueberries; honeybees cannot; bumblebees are used in greenhouse tomato production worldwide.
- Smith et al., Lancet 2015: complete loss of animal pollinators → global supply −22.9 % fruit, −16.3 % vegetables, −22.1 % nuts and seeds; ~1.42 million excess deaths per year (non-communicable and malnutrition-related diseases).
- "Einstein four years" quote: earliest known appearance May 1965, French periodical *La Vie des Bêtes* (Pierre Pascaud); Einstein died 1955; no evidence he said it (Quote Investigator 2013; Africa Check).
- Alfalfa and clover seed production depends on bees (alfalfa leafcutter bees, honeybees, bumblebees) → livestock forage; cotton yields increase with bee visits.
- Coffee (Coffea arabica) is largely self-pollinating, but bee visits raise fruit set and yield (Klein et al., Proc. R. Soc. B 2003).
- Cacao is pollinated by ~1 mm ceratopogonid midges (Forcipomyia); ~3 of every 1,000 flowers become fruit on plantations (Pollinator Partnership "Chocolate and Pollinators").
- Maoxian County, Sichuan: apple and pear growers hand-pollinate with brushes and pollen pots (one person ≈ 5–10 trees per day) after wild pollinators declined (Partap & Ya, Mountain Research and Development 2012; ChinaFile).
- A strong honeybee colony's foragers can visit millions of flowers per day (tens of thousands of foragers × ~10 trips × 50–100 flowers per trip).
- Honeybee produces ~1/12 teaspoon of honey in her lifetime; edible honey found in ancient Egyptian tombs (Smithsonian). Waggle dance decoded by Karl von Frisch, Nobel Prize 1973.
- Harvard RoboBee: ~80 mg (<0.1 g), flapping-wing flight, mostly tethered power.
- Vanilla hand-pollination technique invented in 1841 by 12-year-old Edmond Albius on Réunion; still used today.
- Main threats to bees: habitat/flower loss, pesticides, parasites and disease, climate change (IPBES 2016 pollinator assessment).
